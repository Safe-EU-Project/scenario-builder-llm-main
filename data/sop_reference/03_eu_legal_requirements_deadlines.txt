NIS2 DIRECTIVE 2022/2555 — ARTICLE 23 REPORTING OBLIGATIONS
=============================================================================

Applicability: Applies to essential entities (public administration is listed as an essential sector under Annex I), including law enforcement agencies operating ICT systems.

Definition of "significant incident" (NIS2 Art. 23(3)):
An incident is significant if it:
(a) Has caused or is capable of causing severe operational disruption of the services or financial loss for the entity concerned, or
(b) Has affected or is capable of affecting other natural or legal persons by causing considerable material or non-material damage

Reporting obligations and deadlines (NIS2 Art. 23(4)):

STEP 1 — EARLY WARNING: Within 24 hours of becoming aware of significant incident
- Notify the national CERT/CSIRT or competent authority
- State whether the incident is suspected to be caused by unlawful or malicious action
- Indicate whether cross-border impact is suspected

STEP 2 — INCIDENT NOTIFICATION: Within 72 hours of becoming aware of significant incident
- Update the early warning with:
  - Initial assessment of incident including severity and impact
  - Indicators of compromise (if available)
  - Update on suspected cause (unlawful/malicious action)

STEP 3 — INTERMEDIATE REPORT: On request from national CERT/CSIRT
- Provide status updates as requested by the national authority

STEP 4 — FINAL REPORT: Within one month of submission of incident notification
- Detailed description of incident including severity, impact, and likely cause
- Description of measures taken to address the incident
- Any cross-border impact assessed

Additional NIS2 obligations for essential entities:
- Art. 21: Risk management measures including incident response plans and CIRT capability
- Art. 20: Management body responsible for approving cybersecurity measures and overseeing their implementation
- Art. 32-33: Supervisory authority can impose administrative fines for non-compliance

Penalty for non-compliance (NIS2 Art. 34):
- Essential entities: up to EUR 10,000,000 or 2% of total worldwide annual turnover (whichever is higher)

EU GDPR 2016/679 — ARTICLES 33-34: PERSONAL DATA BREACH NOTIFICATION
=============================================================================

Definition of "personal data breach" (GDPR Art. 4(12)):
"A breach of security leading to the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorised disclosure of, or access to, personal data transmitted, stored or otherwise processed."

GDPR Article 33 — Notification to supervisory authority:
DEADLINE: Without undue delay and, where feasible, NOT LATER THAN 72 HOURS after having become aware of the breach.
This 72-hour deadline is ABSOLUTE. If notification is made after 72 hours, the reasons for the delay must be provided.

Article 33 notification must include (where possible):
(a) Nature of the personal data breach (categories and approximate number of data subjects affected, categories and approximate number of records concerned)
(b) Name and contact details of the Data Protection Officer
(c) Likely consequences of the personal data breach
(d) Measures taken or proposed to be taken to address the breach, including measures to mitigate possible adverse effects

If all information is not available within 72 hours, initial notification should be made and information provided in phases.

GDPR Article 34 — Communication to data subjects:
When to notify data subjects: When the breach is likely to result in a HIGH RISK to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.
DEADLINE: Without undue delay (no specific hour limit, but should be as prompt as possible)

Notification to data subjects NOT required when:
(a) Appropriate technical and organisational protection measures were implemented (encryption, pseudonymisation)
(b) The controller has taken subsequent measures ensuring the high risk no longer materialises
(c) Notification would involve disproportionate effort (public communication acceptable instead)

GDPR Article 33(5) — Record keeping:
The controller shall document any personal data breaches, comprising the facts relating to the personal data breach, its effects and the remedial action taken.

Applies to law enforcement agencies processing civilian personal data. Note: Law enforcement processing for criminal investigations falls under the EU Law Enforcement Directive (LED) 2016/680, not GDPR.

EU LAW ENFORCEMENT DIRECTIVE (LED) 2016/680 — KEY OBLIGATIONS
=============================================================================

Applicability: Applies specifically to processing of personal data by competent authorities (including LEAs) for the purposes of:
- Prevention, investigation, detection, or prosecution of criminal offences
- Execution of criminal penalties

Key obligations relevant to cyber incident response:

Article 29 — Security of processing:
- Implement appropriate technical and organisational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk
- Includes protection against unauthorised or unlawful processing, accidental loss, destruction, or damage

Article 30 — Notification of personal data breach to supervisory authority:
- Similar structure to GDPR Art. 33 but applies to law enforcement personal data
- Deadline: Without undue delay (72-hour guidance applies by analogy)
- Must notify supervisory authority unless breach is unlikely to result in risk to rights and freedoms

Article 31 — Communication to data subject:
- Notify data subject when breach likely results in high risk to rights and freedoms
- Subject to restrictions if notification would compromise criminal investigations

Interaction between GDPR and LED in LEA cyber incidents:
- Personal data of civilians processed for non-law enforcement purposes: GDPR applies
- Personal data processed in context of criminal investigations: LED applies
- In practice, a breach may trigger BOTH GDPR and LED notifications depending on which databases are affected

BUDAPEST CONVENTION ON CYBERCRIME (ETS 185) — KEY ARTICLES FOR LEGISLATIVE MAPPING
=============================================================================

The Budapest Convention provides the primary international legal framework for cybercrime criminalisation and cross-border cooperation.

Article 2 — Illegal access:
Criminalises intentional access to a computer system without right.
Relevance: Covers all intrusion incidents (successful unauthorised access).

Article 3 — Illegal interception:
Criminalises intentional interception of non-public computer data transmissions.
Relevance: Covers network sniffing, man-in-the-middle attacks.

Article 4 — Data interference:
Criminalises intentional damaging, deletion, deterioration, alteration, or suppression of computer data without right.
Relevance: Covers ransomware (encryption of data), data destruction, data manipulation.

Article 5 — System interference:
Criminalises intentional serious hindering of computer system functioning without right.
Relevance: Covers DoS/DDoS attacks, ransomware-induced system unavailability.

Article 6 — Misuse of devices:
Criminalises production, sale, or distribution of tools designed for cybercrime.
Relevance: Covers exploitation frameworks, malware tools, scanning tools used in attacks.

Article 7 — Computer-related forgery:
Criminalises intentional input, alteration, deletion, or suppression of computer data resulting in inauthentic data with intent to be considered authentic.
Relevance: Covers credential forgery, digital document manipulation, timestamp alteration.

Article 8 — Computer-related fraud:
Criminalises intentional causing of loss of property by computer manipulation with fraudulent intent.
Relevance: Covers BEC fraud, ransomware payments obtained under duress, crypto fraud.

Article 9 — Offences related to child pornography:
Criminalises production, distribution, and possession of child pornography via computer systems.
Relevance: Mandatory escalation to Europol EC3 and national authority.

Article 11 — Attempt and aiding or abetting:
Criminalises attempt to commit any of the above offences, and aiding or abetting.
Relevance: Covers intrusion attempts (even if unsuccessful), tools used to facilitate attacks.

Article 35 — 24/7 Network:
Parties must designate a point of contact available 24/7 for expedited assistance in cybercrime investigations.
Relevance: LEAs must know their national 24/7 point of contact for Budapest Convention mutual legal assistance requests.

LEERP — EU LAW ENFORCEMENT EMERGENCY RESPONSE PROTOCOL
=============================================================================

Source: Council of the European Union, document ST-6527-2025
Status: EU framework for coordinating law enforcement responses to major cross-border cyber incidents

LEERP Activation Criteria:
The protocol is activated when a cyber incident meets ALL of the following:
1. The incident affects or is likely to affect two or more EU Member States
2. The impact is significant (operational disruption to critical infrastructure or law enforcement capability)
3. The incident has a confirmed or suspected criminal dimension requiring law enforcement coordination

LEERP Coordination Structure:
- Lead Coordination Body: Europol EC3 (European Cybercrime Centre)
- National Points of Contact: Each Member State designates a 24/7 contact point for LEERP activation
- Communication Channel: SIENA (Secure Information Exchange Network Application)
- Coordination Meetings: Virtual or physical crisis coordination meetings convened by EC3

LEERP Escalation Levels:
Level 1 — National Response: Incident handled by national CERT/CSIRT and national LEA without cross-border activation
Level 2 — LEERP Early Warning: EC3 notified, Member States put on standby
Level 3 — LEERP Coordination: EC3 actively coordinates multi-country response, SIENA channel activated
Level 4 — LEERP Crisis: Full activation including EU-level coordination, potential European Council notification

LEERP reporting to Europol EC3:
- Initial notification: As soon as cross-border or criminal dimension is confirmed or reasonably suspected
- Via SIENA: All information sharing must use SIENA for security classification
- Content: Incident description, affected systems, indicators of compromise, jurisdictions affected

LEERP Information Classification:
All LEERP communications use SIENA classification levels:
- RESTRICTED: Limited distribution (standard operational information)
- RESTRICTED: LEA: Law enforcement agencies only
- SENSITIVE NON-CLASSIFIED: Wider distribution within trusted community

EUROPOL REGULATION 2016/794 — OPERATIONAL OBLIGATIONS
=============================================================================

Article 4 — Objectives:
Europol supports and strengthens Member States' action and their mutual cooperation in preventing and combating serious crime affecting two or more Member States.

Article 7 — Supply of information by Member States:
Member States shall supply Europol with information necessary for the achievement of Europol's objectives.
Relevance for LEAs: When a cyber incident has cross-border criminal dimensions, LEAs MUST share relevant information with Europol.

Article 26 — Processing of personal data:
Europol may process personal data only for specific purposes and with appropriate safeguards.
Relevance: Information shared with Europol EC3 during LEERP activation must comply with data minimisation.
